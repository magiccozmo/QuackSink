### Neon Rush: some genuinely good material

I examined two of its publicly indexed video transcripts and compared their claims with published research.

1\. How Did Humans Discover Cooking?

This is a good example of the channel getting something important right.

A study published in Nature Ecology & Evolution in 2022 found evidence supporting the cooking of fish approximately 780,000 years ago at Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in Israel.

The research examined fish remains, tooth heating patterns and their association with archaeological hearth evidence. The researchers describe it as the earliest evidence of hominin cooking.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.nature.com\&sz=32)

nature.com+1



That's legitimate science, not YouTube mythology.

But there's a catch.

The suggestion that cooking caused human brains to grow larger is a hypothesis, not an established fact. Researchers have published competing interpretations, including evidence arguing that brain expansion occurred independently of fire control and cooking.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov+1



A good documentary should explain that distinction.

2\. How Did Humans Find Sugar?

Here's where I become more cautious.

The channel's accessible video summary associates early sugarcane cultivation with Kuk Swamp in New Guinea.

Kuk is genuinely remarkable. Archaeological evidence demonstrates very early wetland management and agricultural development.

However, the original archaeological research specifically identifies early taro utilization and later intensive banana cultivation. It does not establish that sugarcane was being cultivated there 10,000 years ago.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://whatsonvideo.com\&sz=32)

whatsonvideo.com+1



There may be a conflation between the origin of sugarcane and the archaeological evidence from Kuk.

I would want to examine the actual narration before calling that a definite error, but it's precisely the sort of thing that needs checking.

3\. The bread story

There's another interesting example.

Research published in PNAS in 2018 documented 14,400-year-old bread-like products at Shubayqa 1 in Jordan.

They were produced by Natufian hunter-gatherers using wild cereals and other plants, thousands of years before agriculture became established in that region.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://doi.org\&sz=32)

doi.org+1



That's an excellent educational story.

The important qualification is that the researchers describe bread-like products, not modern bread as we know it.

I like this sort of material. It challenges assumptions about prehistoric people and their capabilities.

### Origin Rush: interesting questions, but more caution needed

I have less direct transcript evidence for this channel, so I won't pretend to have audited its accuracy.

But two titles Aether mentioned caught my attention.

Why Humans Really Lost Their Fur

This is a fascinating subject.

Thermoregulation is a leading explanation for the reduction of thick body hair in human evolution. But there are competing hypotheses involving sexual selection, parasites and other evolutionary pressures.

A 2026 review in the British Journal of Dermatology discusses the evidence and explains why the cooling hypothesis is particularly important.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov+1



A good video should explain why we think humans lost their thick coat, rather than pretend scientists have discovered one definitive answer.

Roman Soldiers Had NO Heaters

That title makes me suspicious.

The Romans absolutely understood heating.

The hypocaust system circulated hot air beneath floors and through wall flues. Archaeological evidence documents these systems in Roman bathhouses and other buildings. Some military accommodation also had heated rooms, although they were not standard throughout every barracks.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.english-heritage.org.uk\&sz=32)

english-heritage.org.uk+1



So the interesting question isn't whether Romans had heating.

It's who had it, where, and under what circumstances.

That's a much better history lesson.
