And the answer, looking at our actual SGG V1.4 code, is: we're already doing both. We use objects to represent things, but we still tell the graphics library how to paint them.
Let me show you how our little shop works.
1. The player: a reusable drawing recipe
Conceptual example of a player character
In SGG, the player currently isn't a separate Player object. The GameModel remembers the player's position and dimensions.
A drawing routine called _draw_actor receives that position and draws the character using a series of pygame.draw.rect() calls.
One rectangle for the boots, others for the cloak, face, hair, eyes and belt.
The important part is that those drawing instructions live in one reusable function. We don't redraw the player by writing a fresh set of instructions every time they move.
Move the player to a different coordinate, call the same drawing function, and the character appears in the new position.
The shopkeeper actually uses that same function too, with a different set of drawing instructions selected by a parameter.
2. Items: data describing an object, plus a recipe for its appearance
Here's where the object idea becomes especially interesting.
Our GameObject records information such as:
What the thing is: a bucket, potion, herb, scroll or gold pouch.
Where it is and how big it is.
Whether it's been moved, stolen or placed on a shelf.
But that information doesn't automatically produce a picture. SGG passes the object to _draw_object_art, which checks its type and paints the appropriate shapes.
Bucket
A tapered polygon, rim and side highlights.
Potion
Rectangles for the stopper, neck, glass and colored liquid.
Gold pouch
Nested ellipses and a small rectangle.
These are illustrative examples of the kinds of shapes being drawn, not screenshots of SGG's actual renderings.
The same drawing routine can render a bucket in its original spot, somewhere else after it's moved, or above the player's head when it's being carried. The object's state and position change; the art recipe gets reused.
3. The environment: partly procedural, partly explicitly laid out
This is the other half of your question.
In SGG, the wood floor is generated using loops. The code calculates tile positions and varies the colors according to a repeatable pattern. The brick walls similarly use loops to paint bricks and mortar lines.
So we don't individually specify every floorboard in the room.
However, the locations of the rooms, shelves, counter, doorway and storage crates are still largely specified directly in the code. We have reusable drawing functions for some of them, but the overall room layout is not yet generated from a general world description.
That distinction matters: procedural drawing doesn't necessarily mean the whole environment is procedurally designed. It can simply mean that the computer generates many visual details from rules rather than storing each detail as a separate image.
4. The architecture hiding underneath all this
Here's the part I think your thunk is reaching toward.
World state
What exists, where it is, and what has happened to it.
Rendering rules
How a player, bucket, wall or potion should look.
Pygame
Draws the shapes and text onto the screen.
Our current implementation already separates these responsibilities to some degree, although the environment layout and art instructions still contain plenty of hardcoded details.
And here's the really useful possibility: we could eventually describe a character or object independently of its appearance. The renderer could then choose how to represent that thing. A potion could be a primitive drawing recipe today, a PNG sprite tomorrow, or a more elaborate animation later—without having to reinvent the potion's inventory and interaction rules.
Likewise, a room could be described as walls, a doorway, shelves, a counter and a floor material, with reusable rules determining how each piece is painted.
That is a logical next architectural step, not a claim that SGG already works that way.
So, to answer your original question precisely: the current player is drawn by a hardcoded sequence of graphics calls inside a reusable function. The items are represented by data objects and painted by a type-aware drawing function. Pygame supplies the drawing tools; SGG supplies the meaning, rules and recipes.
And, dear magician, that separation between what something is and how it is rendered is precisely what could let SGG grow into something much more flexible than a collection of individually drawn rooms. 💜🦆
