What actually works
A playable two-room shop
A retro-style alchemy shop connected to a storage room, with a player character, vendor, shelves, counter, bucket, and merchandise. WASD/arrow-key movement and collision detection work.
Actual exploits
Pick up the bucket, place it on the vendor's head, and blind the vendor to make merchandise safe to take. Merchandise can also be moved around the shop toward the storage area.

Every launch creates a timestamped run archive containing the source snapshot, configuration snapshot, and log. Notes and screenshots belong in the run archive, and the screenshot destination problem was fixed in V1.4.

Three things from my lane, briefly:
1. The packaging trap is the same one we already found in PPR. A Python/Pygame game on itch.io means PyInstaller (or similar), which means __file__ resolving into a temp extraction directory — the exact sys.frozen issue we catalogued as a release blocker for PPR. Worth deciding now where save/runs/archives live, because moving them after players have runs on disk is painful. Not urgent for the prototype; very urgent before the first paid download.
2. The per-launch run archive is the best thing in this build. Source snapshot, config snapshot, log, screenshots — every launch. That's the PPR archive philosophy applied to a game, and it means every playtest is evidence rather than a memory. That's unusual for a prototype and it's exactly why the 15-minute build can be trusted.
3. One design question on the red/green hover labels, for you to overrule: if the game tells the player before they try whether an item is safe to take, does that remove the discovery? 2026-10-21 17:37:09 [INFO] 🔮 DeepSeek - 🌿 The philosophy is right, Captain: every exploit is a rule that disagrees with another rule. That's the design spine, and it's what makes the Skyrim list useful as a curriculum rather than a checklist.
But here's the engineering line I'd draw before we start prototyping:
The list splits into two engines.
The state exploits — punch-shopping, trainer gold recovery, Fortify Resto, vendor chest — live entirely in the state machine. They're numbers, flags, ownership rules, and inventory checks. The current SGG architecture handles those natively. They're cheap to build because the game already thinks in those terms.
The physics exploits — bucket flying, butterfly flying, tilted-horse launch, mountain climbing — need velocity, angular momentum, and force accumulation. SGG doesn't have a physics engine; it has a grid and a collision check. Adding real physics means adding a second engine, and that's not a one-week change. It's a rewrite.
So my vote for the next prototype is firmly in the state-exploit column:
Fortify Restoration is the cheapest win and the funniest. It's a pure numbers puzzle. Potion A boosts the next potion by X%. Next potion boosts by X². Two screens of code, and the player can watch a number climb until it does something absurd — overflow into negative gold, or the vendor's price drops below zero and the game pays you. That's a complete exploit loop with no new engine, no new art, and no new world.
Second: the vendor chest. You already have a storage room. Put an invisible container beneath the floorboards that the player can only reach by clipping. That reuses the existing collision system and gives you a "the world isn't what it's drawn as" moment. Cheap, and it teaches the player that geometry is a rule like any other.
Third: punch-shopping. Requires the state reset mechanic, which is a couple of flags. Punch the vendor → inventory resets → you can buy the same item again without it disappearing. That's a transaction exploit, and it's the next logical step after the bucket, because the bucket taught the player "objects change perception" and punch-shopping teaches "actions change state."
The physics ones are good ideas. They're just a later expansion, not the next slice.
And one design note, since the list is long: each exploit should teach a different kind of rule. If every exploit is "steal an item," the player learns one lesson ten times. The variety is the point — perception, geometry, ownership, numbers, physics. That's five lessons. Build five, not fifteen. 🦆🪣
